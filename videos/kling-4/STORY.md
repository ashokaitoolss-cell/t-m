# FIRST LIGHT

*A story about the night Kling 4.0 opens.*

## 1. The street

It has rained on the town since the afternoon. Nothing moves on the corner except the water,
running down the gutters and sliding over the cobbles under the one street lamp that turns
everything cold blue.

On the corner stands a small cinema that has seen better decades. The marquee is dark and the
letter board above the door is empty.

A small figure runs across the street with a navy coat held over her head like a tent, boots
slapping through the puddles. She doesn't go to the front doors. She goes round the side, to a
narrow door with no sign.

It opens before she can knock.

Her grandfather stands in the doorway: tall, stooped, a charcoal cardigan, reading glasses hanging
on a cord. Behind him is warm yellow light. She ducks under his arm without stopping. He looks once
up the empty street, as if checking the world is still there, then shuts the door. The rain goes
quiet.

They climb the iron stairs, her boots taking them two at a time and dripping water on every step.
His steps follow, slower and heavier.

## 2. The booth

The projection booth is barely bigger than a wardrobe: bare brick, a low ceiling, one caged lamp on
the wall. The projector takes up half the room. It is cast iron, the height of a man, with two big
reels on its arms. On the shelves sit film cans stacked up over fifty years, dented and rusted at
the rims.

Except one. A clean silver can arrived this morning. Its label faces the wall.

He hangs her dripping coat on a nail, and she climbs onto a crate to see.

He opens the new can and lifts out the reel as if it might be hot. Then his hands go to work,
feeding the film through the gate, round the sprockets and through the loops. His fingers are thick
and scarred at the tips from a lifetime of this. He doesn't look at them. He's never had to.

Near the end he stops, one loop left undone. He looks at her and holds out the loose end of the
film.

**"Your turn."**

She takes it. Her hands are small and unsure. The film slips off the sprocket. She breathes, sets
it again and presses, and the teeth catch with a tiny click.

He isn't watching her face. He's watching her hands. His thumb rubs across his own fingertips, as
if he's the one threading it.

## 3. The beam

The switch is old black bakelite, the paint worn away where thumbs have turned it for decades. Her
finger hovers over it. She looks back. Out of focus in the dark behind her, he nods once.

She turns it.

Down in the auditorium, three hundred empty seats sit in the dark. Then the beam comes straight out
of the little port window and across the whole room, dust turning slowly inside it. The projector
starts its chatter, the sound a cinema makes when it's alive.

Through the port glass, the flicker lights both their faces. She stares at the screen with her
lips parted, not breathing. He isn't looking at the screen at all. He's looking at her.

## 4. Now showing

Outside, the rain hasn't stopped and the corner is still empty. It's the same street lamp and the
same wet cobbles we saw when she ran across.

Then the marquee starts to wake. The bulbs warm up a few at a time, amber against all that blue,
until the letters are lit:

**KLING 4.0**
**NOW SHOWING**

Faintly, through the wall, the projector keeps running.

*Black.*

*Kling 4.0.*
